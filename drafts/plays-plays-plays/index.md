Ingrid and I booked several plays in Chichester's Festival season this year as a little adventure. The season offered a nice range of plays, from premieres to classic musicals, and, of course, a bit of Shakespeare. I'd also never been to the Minerva theatre, so we made sure to see at least one there.

This is a review of all six plays that we saw because I've been unable to write the posts as things went along. Sometimes you reflect for a while and realise you have more to say about a play. Sometimes you just want to wait so you don't give away spoilers.

<!--more-->

## Magic

_Magic_ is a fictional telling of the friendship between master illusionist Harry Houdini and Sir Arthur Conan-Doyle, famed creator of Sherlock Holmes. It is known that the two men exchanged letters in real life but there is no record of them meeting. This play imagines their friendship as being driven by a mutual interest in spiritualism, the notion that the dead can contacted by a medium who can relay messages to and from the world of the living.

As the play progresses, it becomes clear that Houdini is in fact more driven by the need to show that spirtualist practice is a sham, driven by his own experiences and those of his mother. Conan-Doyle, reeling from the deaths of his sons in the first world war, is all the more adamant that it is all real. This generates a feud between the two men, which is not fully resolved by the end of the play.

There were some good performances and the stage magic was a lot of fun. I liked the mixture of genuine close-up magic (the stuff you know there is a trick to but you can't work it out in the moment) and the slightly camp, obvious tricks that were used during the scene changes. There is also a scene set during a seance that was genuinely sppoky and a subsequent scene in which the same medium is debunked is also very entertaining. 

But in the end it felt like there wasn't much story there. Add to this the fact that many aspects of Conan-Doyle's life in particular have been moved around and made up for narrative expediency and I'd say I was glad we saw this one first. 

## Eclipse

_Eclipse_ is set in the kitchen of a house in Devon belonging to a famous children's author. He's never seen throughout the play as he's in the back room dying. Instead we meet his two adult children, their partners, and additional characters such as support nurses, the GP, and the postman. Everyone is trying to hold things together and there are secrets and tensions building between everybody. Everyone is so determined not to talk about a dying man, they would much rather talk about all the other things they've been avoiding instead.

The stage design for this play was fantastic. Quite the introduction to the Minerva! Walking in was quite amazing and it was hard to resist not taking a good look at the set, right down to the photos and the empty boxes of Go Cat. There was a garden over the other side complete with a washing line. I think we got the better view of everything from our side of the stage but it did obscure our sight lines a little when it came to the garden scenes. 

The way I've described the plot makes _Eclipse_ seem quite heavy, but it was compassionate to its characters and there was a vein of humour throughout. Building up to the final moments of the play where the father dies, there were some great lines explaining the name of the play. As my parents get older, I find myself thinking about these things more often and I found it to be a helpful to watch this play as a sort of thought experiment. The set design meant that it was like watching an episode of a TV series, but at the same time the nuances and feelings  you experience in the moment from having it performed in front of you make it something else entirely.
